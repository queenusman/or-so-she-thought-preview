Or So She Thought is a psychological novel set in 1990s Norwyn, where duty is inherited, futures are often arranged before they are questioned, and knowing what you want can become difficult when you have spent your life learning what you are supposed to want.

The World

EXPLORE THE KINGDOM OF NORWYN

A kingdom shaped by inheritance, tradition, commerce, and the people who have learned how to live within them.

Norwyn is a kingdom of contrasts.

The capital is busy with commerce, government, diplomacy, and social life. The palace exists within the capital but operates according to rules of its own. Beyond the city, the mountains preserve ways of life shaped by colder weather, difficult geography, and generations of communal work.

Across the border lies Valenne, a neighboring kingdom whose relationship with Norwyn has been built through treaties, trade, diplomacy, and obligations older than Elizabeth herself.

These places are part of the same region, but they do not produce the same kind of life.

Elizabeth has spent most of her life inside one version of Norwyn. The further she moves through it, the more she begins to notice how many versions there are.

01 — THE CAPITAL

WHERE THE KINGDOM MEETS ITSELF

Norwyn's capital was built along stone quays beside the river, where commerce, government, and public life have grown around one another for generations.

The waterfront begins early. Barges arrive carrying goods from elsewhere in the kingdom and beyond it. Workers unload cargo while merchants, clerks, and drivers begin the day's business. Further inland, government offices fill with civil servants. Shops open along the commercial streets. Newspapers appear at kiosks and cafés. Buses move through the older avenues, sharing the roads with cars that belong to a newer decade.

The city changes gradually as one district gives way to another. The buildings around the government quarter are formal and imposing. Commercial streets are louder, with shopfronts, restaurants, banks, hotels, and offices occupying buildings that have often served several purposes over their lifetimes. Residential neighborhoods range from large private houses and carefully maintained avenues to crowded districts where ordinary families live much closer together.

The palace sits within this city, but it does not belong entirely to it. The people of the capital know the royal family exists behind its walls. They see them at ceremonies, read about them in newspapers, hear about them through television broadcasts and conversations, and sometimes encounter the consequences of decisions made inside rooms they will never enter.

The capital has learned to live alongside the monarchy. For most people, it is not extraordinary. It is simply part of the city.

THE WATERFRONT

The stone quays are one of the oldest working parts of the capital. Commerce, river trade, workers, goods, connection between regions.

Goods arrive here before making their way into warehouses, shops, factories, restaurants, and private homes. The waterfront is practical rather than decorative: stone, iron, warehouses, loading areas, carts, vehicles, workers, river traffic, and businesses that depend on the movement of goods.

It is also one of the places where different parts of Norwyn meet. A shipment from the mountains can pass through the same streets as imported goods from Valenne. A merchant can conduct business within sight of a government building. A royal decision about trade can eventually become something as ordinary as the price of a product in a shop.

The river connects more of Norwyn than the people standing beside it necessarily realize.

THE GOVERNMENT QUARTER

Ministries, civil servants, courts, and institutions responsible for the ordinary administrative work of the state.

Its buildings are formal because government is meant to appear permanent.

Inside them, however, much of the work is ordinary: clerks sort documents, officials attend meetings, ministers receive reports, budgets are discussed, letters are answered, and agreements are reviewed.

The kingdom is maintained through thousands of such decisions.

THE COMMERCIAL DISTRICT

Banks, shops, hotels, restaurants, and the places where the capital's wealth becomes visible.

Banks stand beside shops. Hotels serve diplomats and visiting businessmen. Restaurants fill at lunchtime. Department stores display goods that would have been difficult to obtain in previous generations.

There are expensive establishments where wealth is expected and ordinary businesses where people come simply because they need something.

The capital does not divide neatly into rich and poor. Different kinds of wealth exist beside one another.

THE RESIDENTIAL DISTRICTS

Affluent neighborhoods, ordinary families, and different experiences of domestic life in the same city.

Some neighborhoods are quiet and affluent, with large houses, gardens, private schools, and established families. Others are densely populated, with apartments, small shops, schools, markets, and streets where people know one another because they have little choice but to share the same spaces.

The city changes depending on who is looking at it. A palace resident, a government clerk, a shopkeeper, a student, and a dock worker can all live in the same capital without experiencing quite the same city.

02 — THE PALACE

WHERE AUTHORITY BECOMES ORDINARY

The palace is the seat of the royal family and the center of ceremonial life in Norwyn. Its public rooms are designed for occasions that make authority visible: state dinners, diplomatic receptions, royal announcements, formal visits, ceremonies, and celebrations.

But the palace is not only the rooms shown to visitors. Behind the ceremonial spaces are private apartments, family rooms, offices, kitchens, staff corridors, storage rooms, gardens, archives, and the countless practical spaces required to keep a large household functioning.

There is a difference between the palace people visit and the palace people live in. Elizabeth knows the second one. She knows which doors are used by guests and which are used by staff. She knows which rooms require formality and which allow people to relax. She knows how quickly a conversation can change when someone important enters the room.

She has learned these things gradually. Nobody sat her down and explained all of them. She simply grew up knowing.

THE STATE ROOMS

Official dinners, receptions, diplomatic meetings, ceremonies, and royal representation.

Everything in these rooms has a purpose. Where people sit matters. Who enters first matters. Who speaks first matters.

What is displayed on a table, wall, or mantel can carry meaning even when nobody mentions it aloud. The rooms are designed to make continuity visible.

THE ROYAL LIBRARY

Books, records, private reading, and the space associated with Elizabeth's intellectual life.

Books accumulate across generations. Official documents sit alongside private reading. Correspondence may be kept nearby. Family records and political material can exist within the same walls.

For Elizabeth, the library is one of the places where the palace feels less like an institution and more like a home. That distinction is not always easy to maintain.

THE PRIVATE WING

Bedrooms, sitting rooms, private studies, and family spaces removed from performance.

The private wing contains the rooms in which the royal family can temporarily stop performing the public version of themselves.

It is where conversations happen without an audience. It is also where some of the most important decisions are made.

THE HOUSEHOLD

Staff, routines, invisible labor, and the accumulated knowledge that sustains the residence.

Household staff manage meals, clothing, schedules, rooms, correspondence, gardens, transport, security, and countless details that most visitors never notice.

Some staff have worked for the family for years. They know routines. They recognize moods. They know which family members prefer silence in the morning and which expect conversation. They know when an event has gone badly before anyone says so.

Their knowledge is part of the palace's invisible structure.

03 — VALENNE

THE KINGDOM BEYOND THE BORDER

Valenne is Norwyn's neighboring kingdom and one of its most important political and economic partners. Its relationship with Norwyn began long before Elizabeth was born. Treaties connect the two kingdoms. Trade connects their businesses. Diplomacy connects their governments. Previous agreements continue to shape decisions being made in the present.

Elizabeth's proposed marriage therefore does not exist in isolation. It is personal. It is also part of a relationship between two countries that has been developing for generations.

Valenne has its own history, institutions, customs, social hierarchy, and expectations. It is not simply another version of Norwyn, and Edward is not simply a man who happens to come from across the border. He belongs to a different political and social world.

VALENNE'S GOVERNMENT

Governing institutions and the unique balance between monarchy, executive power, and public life.

Its political priorities do not always correspond perfectly with Norwyn's. That difference matters when agreements are negotiated.

A treaty can benefit both countries while benefiting them in different ways.

VALENNE'S ECONOMY

Trade, industry, manufacturing, and financial interests that extend far beyond royal households.

Businesses depend on agreements between the kingdoms. Financial institutions follow political developments. Industries benefit from stable relations.

A royal marriage can therefore attract attention for reasons that have little to do with romance.

04 — THE MOUNTAINS

A DIFFERENT NORWYN

The mountains are part of Norwyn, but they can feel like another country. The weather is colder. Roads are more difficult. Villages are smaller. Work is more visible because survival in a difficult landscape leaves little room for pretending that things maintain themselves.

Wood has to be gathered. Buildings have to be repaired. Food has to be stored. Animals have to be cared for. People depend on one another.

The mountains have preserved ways of life that have changed more slowly than those of the capital.

But they have not preserved a world without rules. They simply have different ones.

THE LANDSCAPE

Steep slopes, pine forests, granite valleys, rocky paths, and roads blocked during colder months.

Distance matters here. A place that looks close on a map may take hours to reach.

Weather can change plans. Geography shapes what people can build, grow, transport, and afford to waste.

THE WORK

Woodworking, farming, animal care, weaving, repair, trade, and seasonal survival labor.

Work is closely connected to survival. Some work belongs to individuals; some work belongs to everyone.

A difficult task can become a community task simply because it is easier to complete together.

Dramatis Personae

MEET THE PEOPLE OF NORWYN

Elizabeth's life is shaped by the people around her, but none of them can be reduced to what they want from her.

They love her.
They need things from her.
They protect her.
They misunderstand her.
Sometimes they do several of these things at once.

ELIZABETH

Elizabeth has spent most of her life knowing what is expected of her.

She is a daughter, a royal, a student, and a future bride. She has learned how to read a room, anticipate a response, and understand what a situation requires of her.

For most of her life, this has been useful.
It has also made certain questions harder to answer.

ANNE

Queen Anne is Elizabeth's mother.

She has spent years preparing her daughter for a life in which very little can be treated as purely personal. She knows what the position requires, what the kingdom expects, and what can happen when someone enters that world unprepared.

She believes preparation is a form of protection.
She does not always recognize when the two begin to become difficult to separate.

EDWARD OF VALENNE

Edward enters Elizabeth's life as the man she is expected to marry.

He is intelligent, observant, politically aware, and more complicated than the arrangement between them initially suggests.

The marriage has consequences beyond the two people expected to enter it.
That does not make what exists between them less personal.

ELIAS

Elias comes from a world outside the palace, but his work brings him into places Elizabeth has spent most of her life taking for granted.

He is observant, independent, and not easily impressed by the distinctions that matter inside the royal household.

His relationship with Elizabeth develops gradually, shaped by conversations, disagreements, shared curiosity, and circumstances neither of them initially understands very well.

There is more to his connection to the palace than Elizabeth knows.

ISABEL

Isabel is Elizabeth's younger sister, and although they were raised within the same royal household, they have not experienced it in quite the same way.

She understands the expectations placed on their family, the responsibilities that come with their position, and the small rules that govern life inside the palace.

She is close to Elizabeth, but being close to someone does not mean seeing the world in exactly the same way.

MAMA ELENA

Mama Elena lives far from the world Elizabeth has known.

Her life is shaped by work, community, necessity, and expectations of a different kind.

Elizabeth does not find a place without rules.
She finds people whose rules she has never learned.

BEHIND THE NOVEL

WHERE IT BEGAN

The Making of Or So She Thought

Or So She Thought did not arrive fully formed.

There was a version of the story before there was this one. There were characters who belonged to earlier versions of it, places that existed before I understood what they were for, scenes that seemed essential until they weren't, and questions I thought I was writing about before realizing I had been asking a different question all along.

That is probably true of most novels. The finished book creates the illusion that it was always heading toward itself. But novels are not usually discovered in that order. They are built through accumulation, revision, disagreement, accident, and the slow realization that the story you thought you were writing is no longer the most interesting one available.

Or So She Thought changed because I kept following those changes.

Every novel has an earlier version of itself. Sometimes that earlier version survives in obvious ways: a character's name, a scene, a location, a discarded chapter. Sometimes it survives more quietly. A question remains even after the story that produced it has disappeared.

Or So She Thought began with a different story. Elizabeth was not initially the person I thought she would become, and the novel did not begin with the particular question that eventually came to define it.

At first, I was more interested in the events. Then I became more interested in the person experiencing them. That changed everything. A plot can tell you what happens to someone. A character can make you ask why it matters to them.

Norwyn could not exist simply because Elizabeth needed somewhere to live. The more I wrote, the more obvious that became.

A fictional world becomes thin when everything inside it appears to have been arranged for the protagonist. Norwyn had to exist before Elizabeth began questioning it. Its institutions needed histories. Its traditions needed reasons. Its inequalities needed consequences. Its ordinary people needed lives that did not depend on whether Elizabeth was paying attention.

Elizabeth was probably the character who changed the novel most. The easiest version of her story would have been familiar: a young woman grows up inside a powerful institution. She discovers that the life prepared for her is not the life she wants. She resists it. She leaves. She becomes free.

The more I understood Elizabeth, the less convincing that version became. She could love her family and still be hurt by them. She could benefit from privilege and still question the system that gave it to her. She could leave and still carry parts of the life she had left behind.

Titles can make a story sound more certain than it is. Or So She Thought did the opposite. It left something unresolved.

The phrase belongs to the space between what a person believes and what they later discover was incomplete. It does not necessarily mean that the person was wrong. That distinction mattered to me.

Looking back, the strangest part of writing a novel is how obvious the finished version can make the process look. The final story has a shape. The earlier versions did not.

Perhaps that is one of the strange privileges of writing fiction. You begin by deciding what happens. Eventually, if you stay with it long enough, the more difficult task becomes noticing what the story keeps asking you to look at.

THE QUESTIONS BEHIND THE STORY

HOW DO YOU KNOW WHAT YOU WANT WHEN YOU WERE TAUGHT WHAT TO WANT?

A person can be influenced by family, culture, class, circumstance, and expectation without their desires becoming false.
So where does influence end and choice begin?

CAN SOMEONE LOVE YOU AND STILL DECIDE TOO MUCH OF YOUR LIFE?

Love does not always arrive separately from power.
Sometimes the person protecting you is also the person deciding what you need protection from.

WHAT HAPPENS WHEN NOBODY HAS TO TELL YOU NO?

The deepest rules are sometimes the ones we learn well enough to enforce ourselves.

NOTES FROM THE NOVEL

Fragments, questions, discoveries, and notes from the process of writing the book.

WHERE OR SO SHE THOUGHT REALLY BEGAN

On the story that came before Elizabeth's story, and the moment one character quietly became more interesting than the novel I had originally planned.

Elizabeth began as a secondary character in a broader historical dispute. But during the drafting of an early dining scene, her quiet compliance began to read not as obedience, but as an active, taxing internal labor. That labor became the true center of the work.

THE MENDED BLOUSE

On one of the smallest objects in the novel, and why Elizabeth repairing something badly became more important than I expected.

In Chapter 05, Elizabeth attempts to repair a seam by hand. For someone whose wardrobe has always been invisible, handled by royal dressers, touching needle to linen and failing to produce perfection is her first brush with manual honesty.

WHY ELIAS HAD TO LIVE

On refusing to let tragedy make Elizabeth's choices too easy.

Killing Elias would have turned Elizabeth's departure into a mourning ceremony or a martyrdom. Letting him survive forced her to decide whether to leave based on who she was, not who she had lost.

BUILDING A PALACE NOBODY THINKS IS A PRISON

On writing a world where control can genuinely feel like care to the person doing the controlling.

Queen Anne does not hate Elizabeth; she is deeply devoted to her preservation. That tenderness makes the cage impossible to see from within.
The Author

ABOUT QUEEN USMAN

A novelist interested in how people become who they are.

Queen Usman writes fiction about the things that shape people before they fully understand they are being shaped—family, desire, expectation, relationships, power, circumstance, and repetition.

Her novel, Or So She Thought, is a psychological novel set in 1990s Norwyn. It follows Elizabeth, a young woman whose future has been carefully prepared for her, and the increasingly difficult question of how much of that future she has actually chosen.
