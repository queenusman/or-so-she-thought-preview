The Premise

An introduction to the story.

In Norwyn, an island kingdom ruled by a royal family, a person's future can be decided long before they are old enough to question it.

Princess Elisabeta has spent her life preparing for hers.

At twenty-two, she is the eldest daughter of Queen Ecaterina and heir to the throne. She has been educated in governance, raised according to the expectations of the royal household and taught to understand the principles that hold her country together. Her engagement to Alexandru of Valenne is another part of the future waiting for her.

It is a life she understands. Its responsibilities have been explained to her, its expectations are familiar, and she has learned to see the reasoning behind the arrangements that govern it.

But understanding why things are done a certain way does not always mean knowing what those arrangements mean for the people living under them.

Beyond the palace, Norwyn is full of people with their own ambitions, difficulties, loyalties and private concerns. Their lives are shaped by circumstances very different from Elisabeta's, and the decisions made in the name of order do not always look the same from where they stand.

As Elisabeta encounters more of the world beyond her familiar surroundings, the explanations she has always relied upon begin to meet experiences they cannot so easily contain.

There are questions that cannot be settled by knowing the right principles. And there are parts of a person's life that may have been decided without them ever realising how much they had accepted.

Or So She Thought is a literary psychological novel about identity, family, duty, privilege and the complicated relationship between the lives we inherit and the lives we might have chosen.

It asks a question that belongs to no particular kingdom or royal family:
How much of who we are comes from the choices we have made, and how much from the life we were taught to accept?

THE WORLD OF NORWYN

A Reader's Introduction

An island kingdom with an ancient royal dynasty, Norwyn has its own customs, institutions and way of life. Its Queen holds absolute authority, its Crown passes from mother to daughter, and its capital lies inland, at the centre of the country's political and administrative life.

Beyond the royal palace are cities, towns, rural communities, workshops, farms, ports and settlements connected by trade and transport. People work, study, raise families and make their living in circumstances that differ considerably from one part of the kingdom to another.

The following is a glimpse of the country in which Elisabeta's story unfolds.

The Island and Its Seasons

Norwyn is a large island with a varied landscape. Mountains and forests occupy parts of the country, while farmland, rivers and long coastlines shape others. Its settlements differ in climate, occupation and way of life.

The landscape is closely connected to the work of its people. Farmland supports agriculture, forests supply timber, rivers carry goods towards inland centres, and coastal communities maintain connections with other kingdoms.

Norwyn has three seasons: spring, autumn and winter. There is no summer. The year moves through the thaw, the return of colder weather, the first frost and periods when the rain eases.

The novel opens in winter, when damp stone, cold rooms and the steady work of keeping fires lit are familiar parts of daily life.

Across the water lie other kingdoms, among them Valenne, whose royal family and commercial interests have an important relationship with Norwyn.

The Crown

Norwyn is an absolute monarchy. The Queen is the kingdom's sovereign and holds supreme governing authority.

The Crown passes through the female line, with the eldest daughter serving as heir. Royal children inherit their mother's royal family name. A Queen's husband does not become King; he retains the title of Prince, and the succession remains within the maternal line.

At the beginning of the story, Norwyn is ruled by Queen Ecaterina. Her eldest daughter, Princess Elisabeta, is heir to the throne. Her younger daughter is Princess Ileana.

The monarchy is part of everyday national life, not simply a ceremonial institution. Its responsibilities extend into the administration of the kingdom, its official records and the institutions that carry out its business.

The People of Norwyn

Norwyn formally recognises two classes: royals and non-royals.

Royals belong to the ruling dynasty. Everyone outside it is non-royal.

Within the non-royal population, however, lives differ greatly. Wealth, occupation, education, family circumstances and access to opportunities all influence the kind of life a person can lead.

A prosperous merchant, a university student, a skilled joiner and a poorly paid labourer may share the same formal status without sharing the same advantages.

The distinction between royal and non-royal is an important part of Norwyn's social structure, but it is not the only distinction that shapes people's lives.

The Capital and the Palace

Norwyn's capital is known simply as the Capital. Situated inland, it is the seat of government, the home of the royal family and the location of several of the kingdom's principal institutions.

The Royal Palace is both a residence and a working establishment. Its rooms accommodate the royal family, household staff and official visitors, while its corridors and offices are part of the daily business of the Crown.

Among its familiar spaces are the library, the Blue Drawing Room, the conservatory, the oak-panelled east gallery and the long hall in the west wing.

The palace is grand without being excessively showy. Its rooms are carefully furnished and maintained, and its atmosphere is shaped by beeswax, wool, polished wood and the warmth of its fires.

Behind its formal appearance is a considerable amount of ordinary work. Gardeners tend the grounds, dressers maintain clothing, attendants manage arrangements, and household staff prepare rooms, meals and fires. The palace depends on the people who keep it functioning, many of whom remain outside the attention given to its royal residents.

Culture and Customs

Norwyn's culture draws on French, Spanish, wider European and Cambodian influences. These can be seen in aspects of its architecture, clothing, food, craftsmanship, ceremony and domestic life.

The kingdom has developed its own blend of these influences. Its identity is not confined to the royal household, and its customs are shared and adapted in different ways across the island.

Norwyn favours a particular kind of elegance: grandeur accompanied by restraint. Fine materials, careful workmanship and attention to detail are valued more than unnecessary display. Formality is part of public life, but it is often expressed through precise manners and understated presentation.

Royal occasions follow established conventions. Introductions, seating arrangements, forms of address and official appearances are carefully organised.

The Queen is addressed as Your Majesty, while princesses are addressed as Your Highness.

Education and the Royal Academy

Education in Norwyn takes place in several kinds of institutions, serving royal children and non-royal students from different backgrounds.

One of the kingdom's principal institutions is the Royal Academy of Governance, situated in the Capital. It provides education in governance and administration and prepares students for responsibilities within the kingdom's institutions.

Its students include members of the royal family as well as people from outside the royal class.

An important part of the Academy's teaching is Structural Ideology, a body of thought concerned with order, organisation and the relationship between individual responsibilities and the needs of the whole.

Within this teaching is the doctrine of Alignment. It describes the ideal of a person whose understanding of order has become so well formed that they recognise what serves the whole without needing to be instructed at every step.

Students encounter these principles through lessons, discussions and practical examples. Their understanding is also assessed through formal examinations and academic panels.

Work and Trade

Fabric and flour are among Norwyn's major trades.

The country's agricultural communities contribute to flour production, while its textile workshops produce cloth for domestic use, commerce and the royal household.

Other important forms of work include timber production, joinery, carpentry, construction, gardening, tailoring, transport and administration.

Forests supply timber, and rivers provide routes for moving heavy goods. Coal and timber are carried towards the Capital, where railway sidings and industrial works support the movement and use of these materials.

Norwyn's economy depends on people with many different skills. Some work in family businesses and independent workshops; others are employed by institutions, merchants, households and larger enterprises.

Trade also extends beyond the island. Commercial arrangements between Norwyn and other kingdoms connect ports, businesses and workers across the water.

Food and Everyday Life

Rice, beans and potatoes are common foods in ordinary Norwynian households.

Differences in prosperity can sometimes be seen in the small details of a meal. Buttered bread, for example, is associated with greater comfort and wealth.

The royal household has a more varied dining tradition. Meals may include soup, fish and tea, while formal receptions and state occasions call for more elaborate arrangements.

Daily life is shaped by occupation, household responsibilities, education and the weather. In larger homes, domestic staff undertake much of the work required to maintain the residence, from preparing rooms and serving meals to caring for clothing and keeping fires burning.

The appearance of an orderly household depends on the work that takes place long before its guests arrive.

Clothing and Dress

Clothing in Norwyn reflects occupation, wealth, climate and occasion.

Royal and wealthy women wear carefully made gowns, often using silk and other fine fabrics. Plain cotton is worn beneath formal clothing, while wool provides warmth during the colder months.

Formal occasions call for particular standards of dress. Men may wear black wool, and women wear gloves and carefully chosen accessories.

Household employees wear uniforms suited to their duties.

Fine clothing is valued not only for its appearance but also for its construction, fabric and workmanship. Norwyn's textile traditions are an important part of its cultural and commercial life.

Technology and Transport

Norwyn is a technologically developed kingdom.

Electric lighting, motor cars, telephones, newspapers, press photography, cassette recorders and railways are all part of its world.

These technologies exist alongside older practices. Candles and coal fires remain familiar, handwritten cards are still exchanged, and paper records continue to play an important role in households and institutions.

Motor cars serve private and official journeys, while railways and rivers connect the Capital with other settlements and commercial centres.

The Royal Family and Public Life

The Crown is a visible part of Norwyn's public life.

The royal family attends official visits, charity occasions, receptions, state dinners, public openings and formal ceremonies. Such events are carefully arranged, from the order of arrival to the seating plan and the photographs released to the press.

Royal marriages are also connected to the relationships between kingdoms. They can carry dynastic and diplomatic significance, bringing together families whose positions extend beyond their personal circumstances.

For members of the royal household, public appearances and private responsibilities are closely connected.

Valenne

Across the water from Norwyn lies Valenne, a separate kingdom with its own royal court, educational institutions and commercial interests.

Its southern ports are important to trade and employment. Agreements between the two kingdoms affect businesses, institutions and the people whose livelihoods depend on their arrangements.

Alexandru, Elisabeta's betrothed, is the heir to Valenne. His position makes the relationship between the two royal families an important part of the world surrounding the story.

Institutions and Records

Norwyn has institutions responsible for maintaining official records and administering different aspects of national life.

The Founders' Archive preserves records and is supported by the Founders' Trust, which also organises public occasions such as its annual gala.

The Inspectorate oversees the Timber Inspection Schedule. Under this arrangement, timber entering Norwyn's trade is inspected on four fixed days each year, according to the same standard and using official seals.

The Schedule is regarded as an example of orderly administration because it is designed to apply the same requirements regardless of ownership.

It is also one of the examples used at the Royal Academy when students study the principles of Structural Ideology.

BEYOND THE PALACE

There is a kingdom beyond the royal residence, with people whose lives do not revolve around the Crown.

They have homes to maintain, work to do, relationships to navigate and familiar routines that give structure to their days. They live within a society shaped by its own traditions and distinctions, just as the royal family does.

For the characters of Or So She Thought, Norwyn is more than the place where events happen. It is part of the world that has shaped their understanding of family, position, responsibility and the lives they might lead. To understand the story, it helps to understand the world around it.

The Characters

A Character Guide

The people of Or So She Thought come from different households, professions and positions within Norwynian society. Some belong to the royal family; others are connected to the institutions, businesses and everyday work that keep the kingdom running.

Here are some of the people you will encounter as the story begins.

The Royal Family

Princess Elisabeta

The Heiress

Age: Twenty-two

Position: Eldest daughter of Queen Ecaterina, heir to the throne of Norwyn and betrothed to Alexandru of Valenne.

Raised in the Royal Palace, Elisabeta has spent much of her life preparing for the responsibilities that accompany her position. Her education includes private lessons in Structural Ideology and formal study at the Royal Academy of Governance.

She is courteous, composed and attentive to detail. She chooses her words carefully, often qualifies her statements and is accustomed to explaining her reasoning. She notices small changes in her surroundings and has a particular habit of counting things.

She reads closely, keeps a private notebook and is familiar with the principles that have shaped her education.

As the heir, Elisabeta is expected to represent the continuity of Norwyn's royal dynasty. She has been taught to understand what that responsibility requires, though understanding a responsibility and experiencing it are not always the same thing.

Queen Ecaterina

The Sovereign

Position: Reigning Queen of Norwyn and mother of Princesses Elisabeta and Ileana.

Ecaterina is measured and composed, rarely hurrying to fill a silence. She speaks with precision and tends to explain her reasoning rather than rely on unnecessary commands.

She often wears dark plum gowns with silver detailing and gloves. Her appearance is carefully maintained, and she notices the small details of dress and posture in those around her.

She is accustomed to the responsibilities of the Crown and to the demands of running a royal household.

To her daughters, she is both their mother and the sovereign of their kingdom. The two roles are never entirely separate.

Princess Ileana

The Second Daughter

Position: The Queen's second daughter and Elisabeta's younger sister.

Ileana is quiet, precise and dry in her humour. She is attentive to practical details and often notices things that others overlook.

She is familiar with the organisation of the royal household and regularly works with papers, records and household information.

Although she shares her sister's intelligence, she has a different temperament and occupies a different position within the family.

Safira

The Companion

Position: Elisabeta's cat.

Safira is a familiar presence in the palace. She sleeps beneath tables, settles wherever there is room and follows the movements of the people around her.

She has her own ideas about where she belongs, which do not always coincide with the household's arrangements.

The Court of Valenne

Alexandru of Valenne

The Intended

Position: Heir to the throne of Valenne and Elisabeta's betrothed.

Alexandru is tall and fair, with hair that tends to come loose after a walk across a courtyard in the rain.

He is warm, socially confident and quick with a joke. He remembers small details about people, sometimes imperfectly, and is comfortable in formal company.

He is fond of horses, particularly a grey horse named Marrow, who has an unfortunate refusal to cross water.

Alexandru and Elisabeta first met when she was eighteen. Their engagement forms part of the relationship between the royal families of Norwyn and Valenne.

Ambassador Duvall

The Diplomat

Position: Valenne's ambassador to Norwyn.

A spare, silver-haired man of quiet manner, Duvall represents Valenne at the Norwyn court and attends formal occasions involving the two kingdoms.

The Royal Household

Mr. Corrow

The Tutor

Position: Elisabeta's private tutor.

Mr. Corrow teaches Elisabeta Structural Ideology and conducts her weekly lessons in the palace library.

He is courteous, precise and fond of order. He values careful reasoning and takes particular satisfaction in a pupil who can defend an argument under questioning.

He has taught Elisabeta for four years and is familiar with her academic abilities.

Mrs. Hale

The Dresser

Position: Royal dresser.

Mrs. Hale is responsible for fitting and maintaining the royal family's gowns. She has worked in the household for many years and knows the practical demands of dressing its members.

Direct and observant, she is particular about measurements, fabrics and the condition of every garment. She often works with pins in her mouth and keeps careful records of the gowns entrusted to her.

The Steward

Administration

Position: Senior officer of the royal household.

The steward oversees domestic arrangements and communicates official instructions and information.

Courteous and restrained, he is accustomed to the routines and formalities of palace administration.

The Household Staff

The Workers

The palace employs pages, maids, attendants, dressers, stewards, secretaries, guards, cooks, gardeners and other workers.

Their responsibilities differ, but together they maintain the daily life of the royal residence. An attendant accompanies Elisabeta whenever she leaves the palace.

The Academy & The Fletcher Family

Professor Calloway

The Lecturer

Position: Lecturer at the Royal Academy of Governance.

Professor Calloway teaches the doctrine of Alignment and uses practical examples to explain the principles of Structural Ideology.

He is formal and composed. He carries a folder to the lectern but rarely needs to open it during a lesson.

Lucian Fletcher

The Joiner’s Son

Position: Student at the Royal Academy of Governance and son of Tobias Fletcher, a joiner.

Lucian is broad-shouldered and slightly stooped, with large hands and marked knuckles. He wears a grey jumper mended at both elbows, often keeps a pencil behind his ear and has a pale scar along his jaw.

He is observant, direct and unhurried. He answers plainly, uses dry humour sparingly and is comfortable admitting when he does not know something.

His family is connected to Norwyn's timber trade, and he has practical experience of wood and the work involved in preparing it.

At the Academy, Lucian brings a familiarity with physical work and materials into discussions often concerned with principles and administration.

Tobias Fletcher

The Joiner

Position: Joiner and father of Lucian Fletcher.

Tobias operates a timber yard beside the river. His workshop has undertaken work for the palace, including woodwork for the east wing.

He is broad and grey-haired, with large hands marked by years of manual work. He speaks slowly and has a dry sense of humour.

His work is part of the skilled craftsmanship that supports Norwyn's buildings and businesses.

A Note from the Author

Welcome to Or So She Thought.

This novel takes place in Norwyn, an imagined island kingdom with its own history, institutions, customs and way of life. Although its world draws on a range of historical and cultural influences, Norwyn is a fictional place, shaped by the needs of the story and the people who inhabit it.

At the centre of the novel is Elisabeta, a young woman raised within the royal family and expected to inherit a life already surrounded by responsibilities, traditions and expectations. Through her experiences and relationships, the story explores the complicated ways in which family, upbringing, duty and personal desire can shape a person's understanding of themselves.

I have always been interested in the parts of people that are not immediately visible: the thoughts behind familiar behaviour, the expectations people learn to live with, the small adjustments they make to belong, and the moments when what they have always known begins to look different. These interests found their way into this story.

As you read, you will encounter a world that extends beyond the events of the main narrative. The World of Norwyn, the character introductions and The Norwyn Archives offer additional glimpses into the kingdom, its people and its institutions. They are there for readers who enjoy exploring the details of a fictional world, but they are not required reading. The story can be followed through the chapters alone.

I hope you enjoy discovering Norwyn and getting to know the people who call it home. More than anything, I hope the story gives you something worth thinking about long after you have turned the final page.

Thank you for reading.
Queen Usman
