The World

EXPLORE THE KINGDOM OF NORWYN

A kingdom shaped by inheritance, tradition, commerce, and the people who have learned how to live within them.

Norwyn is a kingdom of contrasts.

The capital is busy with commerce, government, diplomacy, and social life. The palace exists within the capital but operates according to rules of its own. Beyond the city, the mountains preserve ways of life shaped by colder weather, difficult geography, and generations of communal work.

Across the border lies Valenne, a neighboring kingdom whose relationship with Norwyn has been built through treaties, trade, diplomacy, and obligations older than Elizabeth herself.

Elizabeth has spent most of her life inside one version of Norwyn. The further she moves through it, the more she begins to notice how many versions there are.

01 — THE CAPITAL

WHERE THE KINGDOM MEETS ITSELF

Norwyn's capital was built along stone quays beside the river, where commerce, government, and public life have grown around one another for generations.

The waterfront begins early. Barges arrive carrying goods from elsewhere in the kingdom and beyond it. Workers unload cargo while merchants, clerks, and drivers begin the day's business. Further inland, government offices fill with civil servants. Shops open along the commercial streets. Newspapers appear at kiosks and cafés. Buses move through the older avenues, sharing the roads with cars that belong to a newer decade.

THE WATERFRONT

The stone quays are one of the oldest working parts of the capital. Commerce, river trade, workers, goods, connection between regions.

Goods arrive here before making their way into warehouses, shops, factories, restaurants, and private homes. The waterfront is practical rather than decorative: stone, iron, warehouses, loading areas, carts, vehicles, workers, river traffic, and businesses that depend on the movement of goods.

THE GOVERNMENT QUARTER

Ministries, civil servants, courts, and institutions responsible for the ordinary administrative work of the state.

Its buildings are formal because government is meant to appear permanent. Inside them, however, much of the work is ordinary: clerks sort documents, officials attend meetings, ministers receive reports, budgets are discussed, and letters are answered.

02 — THE PALACE

WHERE AUTHORITY BECOMES ORDINARY

The palace is the seat of the royal family and the center of ceremonial life in Norwyn. Behind the ceremonial spaces are private apartments, family rooms, offices, kitchens, staff corridors, storage rooms, gardens, archives, and practical household spaces.

There is a difference between the palace people visit and the palace people live in. Elizabeth knows the second one.

THE STATE ROOMS

Official dinners, receptions, diplomatic meetings, ceremonies, and royal representation.

Everything in these rooms has a purpose. Where people sit matters. Who enters first matters. Who speaks first matters. The rooms are designed to make continuity visible.

THE ROYAL LIBRARY

Books, records, private reading, and the space associated with Elizabeth's intellectual life.

Books accumulate across generations. Official documents sit alongside private reading. For Elizabeth, the library is one of the places where the palace feels less like an institution and more like a home.

Dramatis Personae

MEET THE PEOPLE OF NORWYN

Elizabeth's life is shaped by the people around her, but none of them can be reduced to what they want from her.

They love her.
They need things from her.
They protect her.
They misunderstand her.
Sometimes they do several of these things at once.

ELIZABETH

Elizabeth has spent most of her life knowing what is expected of her.

She is a daughter, a royal, a student, and a future bride. She has learned how to read a room, anticipate a response, and understand what a situation requires of her.

For most of her life, this has been useful. It has also made certain questions harder to answer.

ANNE

Queen Anne is Elizabeth's mother.

She has spent years preparing her daughter for a life in which very little can be treated as purely personal. She believes preparation is a form of protection. She does not always recognize when the two begin to become difficult to separate.

EDWARD OF VALENNE

Edward enters Elizabeth's life as the man she is expected to marry.

He is intelligent, observant, politically aware, and more complicated than the arrangement between them initially suggests. The marriage has consequences beyond the two people expected to enter it.

ELIAS

Elias comes from a world outside the palace, but his work brings him into places Elizabeth has spent most of her life taking for granted.

He is observant, independent, and not easily impressed by the distinctions that matter inside the royal household.

ISABEL

Isabel is Elizabeth's younger sister, and although they were raised within the same royal household, they have not experienced it in quite the same way.

She has her own understanding of what their family owes to the kingdom, and her own reasons for accepting some things Elizabeth has begun to question.

MAMA ELENA

Mama Elena lives far from the world Elizabeth has known.

Her life is shaped by work, community, necessity, and expectations of a different kind. Elizabeth does not find a place without rules; she finds people whose rules she has never learned.

ROWAN

Rowan's influence reaches Elizabeth through a book before it reaches her through memory.

His ideas give language to questions Elizabeth has not yet learned how to ask.

THE MAKING OF THE NOVEL

WHERE IT BEGAN

The Making of Or So She Thought

Or So She Thought did not arrive fully formed.

There was a version of the story before there was this one. There were characters who belonged to earlier drafts, places that existed before I understood what they were for, scenes that seemed essential until they weren't, and questions I thought I was writing about before realizing I had been asking a different question all along.

Novels are built through accumulation, revision, disagreement, accident, and the slow realization that the story you thought you were writing is no longer the most interesting one available. Or So She Thought changed because I kept following those changes.

THE STORY BEFORE ELIZABETH

On the story that existed before Or So She Thought, and the moment Elizabeth became more interesting than the novel I had originally planned.

Every novel begins somewhere else. At first, I was more interested in external events and political maneuvering across borders. Then Elizabeth began to emerge—not as an instrument of the plot, but as a person whose quiet compliance was itself an exhausting form of labor. Once her inner life became the primary question, the entire architecture of the book had to shift to meet her.

THE MENDED BLOUSE

On a small object that unexpectedly became part of Elizabeth's story.

Some objects in fiction are chosen deliberately for their symbolism; others arrive by accident and refuse to leave. In early drafts, the blouse was merely a detail in a domestic scene. But watching someone whose clothes have always been selected, tailored, and maintained by others attempt to fix an imperfect hem became a quiet study in what happens when self-reliance begins with clumsy fingers.

THE TITLE

On how Or So She Thought became the title, and why uncertainty mattered more than certainty.

Titles often attempt to declare what a book is about. Or So She Thought does the opposite—it creates hesitation. It belongs to the narrow space between what a person assumes to be true and what they slowly realize was incomplete. It is not about discovering that everything was a lie; it is about discovering how much more complicated the truth actually is.

BUILDING NORWYN

On creating a kingdom that had to feel lived in before Elizabeth ever began questioning it.

A world cannot feel like a stage set constructed merely for the protagonist's dilemma. Norwyn’s ministries, stone quays, river barges, and mountain passes had to exist as places where ordinary people live their ordinary days. If the rules of the kingdom did not make coherent sense to the people upholding them, Elizabeth's quiet resistance would have cost her nothing.

WRITING ANNE

On writing a mother whose love, certainty, responsibility, and mistakes could not be separated neatly from one another.

The easiest version of Anne would have been an imposing tyrant. But power inside families rarely operates through overt hostility. Anne is driven by duty and a fierce belief that discipline is the highest form of protection. When control looks and feels like genuine care, the boundaries between devotion and constraint become almost impossible to disentangle.

THE SCENES THAT SURVIVED

On the scenes that remained after earlier versions of the novel disappeared.

As a manuscript is rewritten, whole chapters, characters, and subplots fall away. The scenes that endure are rarely the loudest ones. They are the moments of silence: a conversation in a carriage where nothing of consequence is said, an unread letter left on a cedar desk, or a shared glance across a banquet hall that registers an unspoken change.
QUESTIONS THE NOVEL LEAVES BEHIND

BEYOND THE NOVEL

Or So She Thought is a story, but some of the questions it raises do not belong entirely to Elizabeth, Norwyn, or the world of the novel.

They appear in ordinary lives too.

How much of what we call preference was learned?
How much of what feels inevitable was simply repeated?
When does something we were taught become something we genuinely believe?
And what happens when we begin to notice?
THE QUESTIONS

HOW DO YOU KNOW WHAT YOU WANT?

Family, culture, circumstance, expectation, and experience all participate in forming a person.
But influence does not make a desire false.
So where does influence end and choice begin?

CAN A LIFE CHOSEN FOR YOU BECOME YOUR OWN?

We inherit ideas about what a good life should look like.
Sometimes we reject them.
Sometimes we keep them.
Sometimes we cannot tell which happened.

CAN LOVE AND CONTROL EXIST TOGETHER?

The person who knows you best may also be the person who feels most entitled to decide what is best for you.
Where does care become something else?

WHAT HAPPENS WHEN A RULE BECOMES ORDINARY?

Some rules remain visible.
Others become so familiar that nobody thinks to call them rules anymore.

WHEN DOES A RESPONSE BECOME A TRAIT?

A person does something often enough.
Other people begin to expect it.
Eventually, the person may begin to expect it too.

WHAT DO WE CALL CHOICE WHEN THE OPTIONS ALREADY FEEL OBVIOUS?

A decision can feel completely voluntary and still have a long history behind it.
That does not make it meaningless.
It makes its history worth examining.

The Author

ABOUT QUEEN USMAN

A clinical medical student and novelist interested in how people become who they are.

Queen Usman is a Nigerian clinical medical student and novelist. She writes fiction about the things that shape people before they fully understand they are being shaped: family, desire, expectation, relationships, power, circumstance, and repetition.

Her current novel, Or So She Thought, is a psychological novel set in 1990s Norwyn. It follows Elizabeth, a young woman whose future has been carefully prepared for her, as the life she has always understood begins to become less certain.

Her medical training has sharpened her interest in observation, human behavior, and the ways people are shaped by the worlds they inhabit. She is particularly interested in the space between what happens to a person and what that person eventually becomes.

Her essays follow the questions her fiction leaves behind, looking at the ordinary ways people acquire expectations, habits, assumptions, preferences, and ways of seeing.
