Premise

Elisabeta is twenty-two, heir to the crown of Norwyn, and very good at knowing what is expected of her.

In the island kingdom of Norwyn, the crown passes from mother to daughter, and every royal child carries her mother's name. The kingdom is governed by centuries of tradition, carefully maintained institutions and a philosophy that teaches that stability comes from arranging differences so that nothing works against the whole. Its Capital stands inland, among old stone buildings, long galleries and grand residences. There are three seasons and no summer. Fires are lit before dawn, rooms are prepared before their occupants arrive, and an entire world of people work quietly to keep the kingdom running.

Elisabeta has been raised to understand this world. As the eldest daughter of Queen Ecaterina, she has spent her life studying its history, learning the principles of government and preparing for the responsibilities of a future queen. She knows how to answer difficult questions, defend established principles and carry herself with the composure expected of someone who will one day inherit a kingdom. Her engagement to Prince Alexandru of Valenne is another part of that future, one that two kingdoms have spent a year preparing.

It is, by every reasonable measure, a remarkable life. She has a loving family, a kingdom to inherit and a future that appears to have been arranged with considerable care.

And, until recently, she has had little reason to question any of it.

Her mother loves her. Her sister is her closest friend. Her future husband is pleasant and well-meaning. The people around her are not cruel, and nobody seems particularly interested in making her unhappy. If anything, they are looking after her. There are arrangements for her comfort, decisions made in her best interests and expectations that have existed for so long that nobody thinks to explain them.

But a well-run life can conceal a great deal.

It begins with small things. A wire caught on a branch. A seam in a wooden panel. A glass of water that arrives at precisely the right temperature, though she has seen nobody bring it. The details are easily dismissed, yet they leave her with an unfamiliar sense that there is more happening around her than she has been taught to notice.

Beyond the palace, Norwyn is full of lives she has never had to consider closely. People make promises, pursue ambitions, endure disappointments, fall in love and find ways to live within circumstances they did not choose. Their lives are shaped by the same traditions and institutions that have shaped hers, though from positions very different from her own. The kingdom she has always understood from the perspective of its inheritance, its history and its government begins to look different when she considers the people who keep it functioning.

As Elisabeta becomes more attentive to the world around her, familiar explanations become harder to accept without examination. She begins to notice what has been arranged, who has made the arrangements, what they require of others and how little she has ever needed to ask. Even the future she has been preparing for begins to look different when she considers what it means to inherit a life that so many people have helped to decide.

There is no single revelation waiting to overturn everything she knows. No obvious enemy to expose, no simple injustice to correct and no easy distinction between those who care for her and those who expect something from her. The people she loves are part of the world she is beginning to question, just as she is part of theirs.

And somewhere between the answers she has always known and the questions she has only just begun to ask, Elisabeta must confront something she has never had much reason to consider:

Does knowing how to live the life prepared for you mean you have ever known what you wanted from it?

Or So She Thought is a literary psychological novel about inheritance, family, duty, identity and the quiet arrangements that shape a person's life. It is about a kingdom, the people who sustain it, and a young woman beginning to discover that being cared for is not necessarily the same as being known.

A Note from the Author

I have always been interested in the things people rarely think to question. The familiar arrangements of a life. The small expectations that become second nature. The ways we learn to accommodate, to anticipate, to make ourselves comfortable with things we never consciously chose. And how, sometimes, it takes very little to make something we have always known feel suddenly unfamiliar.

I wanted to explore some of these things through fiction, not by offering answers, but by following people closely enough to see what they might not see in themselves. I am drawn to the ordinary moments that reveal more than they appear to: a remark that stays with someone longer than it should, a kindness whose meaning changes with time, a decision made almost without thought that quietly alters the course of something else. The things that seem insignificant until they are no longer.

In writing this novel, I became particularly interested in the relationship between care and understanding, between what is done for us and what is understood about us, and in how easily the two can be mistaken for one another. There is something fascinating about the lives we consider settled, the beliefs we hold without examination, and the moments when neither feels quite as certain as it once did.

I have no particular interest in making every question resolve neatly, or in deciding what a reader ought to think of the people they encounter. Some things are better left a little unsettled. Perhaps there is value in looking at something twice, in allowing an impression to linger long enough for it to become something else.

Thank you for entering this world, for spending your time with its people, and for reading Or So She Thought.

Queen Usman
