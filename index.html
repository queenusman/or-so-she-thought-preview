ABOUT THIS WORLD

Norwyn, its people, places, institutions, histories, and events are fictional creations from Or So She Thought.

The World

EXPLORE THE KINGDOM OF NORWYN

A kingdom shaped by inheritance, tradition, commerce, and the people who have learned how to live within them.

Norwyn is a kingdom of contrasts.

The capital is busy with commerce, government, diplomacy, and social life. The palace exists within the capital but operates according to rules of its own. Beyond the city, the mountains preserve ways of life shaped by colder weather, difficult geography, and generations of communal work.

Across the border lies Valenne, a neighboring kingdom whose relationship with Norwyn has been built through treaties, trade, diplomacy, and obligations older than Elizabeth herself.

These places are part of the same kingdom and the same region, but they do not produce the same kind of life.

Elizabeth has spent most of her life inside one version of Norwyn. The further she moves through it, the more she begins to notice how many versions there are.

THE CAPITAL

WHERE THE KINGDOM MEETS ITSELF

Norwyn's capital was built along stone quays beside the river, where commerce, government, and public life have grown around one another for generations.

The waterfront begins early.
Barges arrive carrying goods from elsewhere in the kingdom and beyond it. Workers unload cargo while merchants, clerks, and drivers begin the day's business. Further inland, government offices fill with civil servants. Shops open along the commercial streets. Newspapers appear at kiosks and cafés. Buses move through the older avenues, sharing the roads with cars that belong to a newer decade.

The city changes gradually as one district gives way to another.
The buildings around the government quarter are formal and imposing. Commercial streets are louder, with shopfronts, restaurants, banks, hotels, and offices occupying buildings that have often served several purposes over their lifetimes. Residential neighborhoods range from large private houses and carefully maintained avenues to crowded districts where ordinary families live much closer together.

The palace sits within this city, but it does not belong entirely to it.
The people of the capital know the royal family exists behind its walls. They see them at ceremonies, read about them in newspapers, hear about them through television broadcasts and conversations, and sometimes encounter the consequences of decisions made inside rooms they will never enter.

The capital has learned to live alongside the monarchy.
For most people, it is not extraordinary.
It is simply part of the city.

THE WATERFRONT

The stone quays are one of the oldest working parts of the capital.

Goods arrive here before making their way into warehouses, shops, factories, restaurants, and private homes. The waterfront is practical rather than decorative: stone, iron, warehouses, loading areas, carts, vehicles, workers, river traffic, and businesses that depend on the movement of goods.

It is also one of the places where different parts of Norwyn meet.

A shipment from the mountains can pass through the same streets as imported goods from Valenne. A merchant can conduct business within sight of a government building. A royal decision about trade can eventually become something as ordinary as the price of a product in a shop.

The river connects more of Norwyn than the people standing beside it necessarily realize.

THE GOVERNMENT QUARTER

The government quarter contains the ministries, administrative offices, courts, and other institutions responsible for the ordinary work of the state.

Its buildings are formal because government is meant to appear permanent.

Inside them, however, much of the work is ordinary.

Clerks sort documents. Officials attend meetings. Ministers receive reports. Budgets are discussed. Letters are answered. Agreements are reviewed.

The kingdom is maintained through thousands of such decisions.

THE COMMERCIAL DISTRICT

The commercial district is where the capital's money becomes visible.

Banks stand beside shops. Hotels serve diplomats and visiting businessmen. Restaurants fill at lunchtime. Department stores display goods that would have been difficult to obtain in previous generations.

There are expensive establishments where wealth is expected and ordinary businesses where people come simply because they need something.

The capital does not divide neatly into rich and poor.

Different kinds of wealth exist beside one another.

THE RESIDENTIAL DISTRICTS

The capital contains many different versions of domestic life.

Some neighborhoods are quiet and affluent, with large houses, gardens, private schools, and established families. Others are densely populated, with apartments, small shops, schools, markets, and streets where people know one another because they have little choice but to share the same spaces.

The city changes depending on who is looking at it.

A palace resident, a government clerk, a shopkeeper, a student, and a dock worker can all live in the same capital without experiencing quite the same city.

THE PALACE

WHERE AUTHORITY BECOMES ORDINARY

The palace is the seat of the royal family and the center of ceremonial life in Norwyn. Its public rooms are designed for occasions that make authority visible: state dinners, diplomatic receptions, royal announcements, formal visits, ceremonies, and celebrations.

But the palace is not only the rooms shown to visitors.

Behind the ceremonial spaces are private apartments, family rooms, offices, kitchens, staff corridors, storage rooms, gardens, archives, and the countless practical spaces required to keep a large household functioning.

There is a difference between the palace people visit and the palace people live in. Elizabeth knows the second one. She knows which doors are used by guests and which are used by staff. She knows which rooms require formality and which allow people to relax. She knows how quickly a conversation can change when someone important enters the room.

She has learned these things gradually.
Nobody sat her down and explained all of them.
She simply grew up knowing.

THE STATE ROOMS

The state rooms are the most visible part of royal life.

They are used for official dinners, receptions, diplomatic meetings, ceremonies, and other occasions where the royal family represents the kingdom.

Everything in these rooms has a purpose.

Where people sit matters.
Who enters first matters.
Who speaks first matters.

What is displayed on a table, wall, or mantel can carry meaning even when nobody mentions it aloud.

The rooms are designed to make continuity visible.

THE ROYAL LIBRARY

The library is quieter than the state rooms and more personal.

Books accumulate across generations. Official documents sit alongside private reading. Correspondence may be kept nearby. Family records and political material can exist within the same walls.

For Elizabeth, the library is one of the places where the palace feels less like an institution and more like a home.

That distinction is not always easy to maintain.

THE PRIVATE WING

The private wing contains the rooms in which the royal family can temporarily stop performing the public version of themselves.

Bedrooms. Sitting rooms. Private studies. Family spaces.

It is where conversations happen without an audience.

It is also where some of the most important decisions are made.

THE HOUSEHOLD

A palace cannot function through the royal family alone.

Household staff manage meals, clothing, schedules, rooms, correspondence, gardens, transport, security, and countless details that most visitors never notice.

Some staff have worked for the family for years.

They know routines.
They recognize moods.

They know which family members prefer silence in the morning and which expect conversation. They know when an event has gone badly before anyone says so.

Their knowledge is part of the palace's invisible structure.

THE ARCHIVES

The royal archives contain records of the family and the kingdom: correspondence, announcements, photographs, treaties, invitations, official documents, and records from previous generations.

The archive preserves what the institution chooses to remember.

It can also reveal what earlier generations were trying to manage, protect, or conceal.

VALENNE

THE KINGDOM BEYOND THE BORDER

Valenne is Norwyn's neighboring kingdom and one of its most important political and economic partners. Its relationship with Norwyn began long before Elizabeth was born. Treaties connect the two kingdoms. Trade connects their businesses. Diplomacy connects their governments. Previous agreements continue to shape decisions being made in the present.

Elizabeth's proposed marriage therefore does not exist in isolation.

It is personal.
It is also part of a relationship between two countries that has been developing for generations.

Valenne has its own history, institutions, customs, social hierarchy, and expectations. It is not simply another version of Norwyn, and Edward is not simply a man who happens to come from across the border.

He belongs to a different political and social world.

VALENNE'S GOVERNMENT

Valenne has its own governing institutions and its own relationship between monarchy, government, and public life.

Its political priorities do not always correspond perfectly with Norwyn's.

That difference matters when agreements are negotiated.

A treaty can benefit both countries while benefiting them in different ways.

VALENNE'S ECONOMY

Trade between Norwyn and Valenne has created interests that extend far beyond the royal families.

Businesses depend on agreements between the kingdoms. Financial institutions follow political developments. Industries benefit from stable relations.

A royal marriage can therefore attract attention for reasons that have little to do with romance.

NORWYN AND VALENNE

The relationship between the kingdoms is built on more than friendship.

There are agreements.
There are obligations.
There are economic interests.
There is history.

And there are things each kingdom would prefer the other not to misunderstand.

EDWARD OF VALENNE

Edward has grown up understanding his position within this relationship.

He knows that marriage can be personal and political at the same time.

He also knows that Elizabeth is being asked to enter a world that existed before she was asked to enter it.

For Elizabeth, understanding Edward eventually means understanding more than the man himself.

She has to understand what comes with him.

THE MOUNTAINS

A DIFFERENT NORWYN

The mountains are part of Norwyn, but they can feel like another country. The weather is colder. Roads are more difficult. Villages are smaller. Work is more visible because survival in a difficult landscape leaves little room for pretending that things maintain themselves.

Wood has to be gathered.
Buildings have to be repaired.
Food has to be stored.
Animals have to be cared for.
People depend on one another.

The mountains have preserved ways of life that have changed more slowly than those of the capital.

But they have not preserved a world without rules.
They simply have different ones.

THE LANDSCAPE

The mountain region is made up of steep slopes, forests, valleys, rocky paths, small settlements, and roads that become difficult during the colder months.

Distance matters here.

A place that looks close on a map may take hours to reach.

Weather can change plans.

Geography shapes what people can build, grow, transport, and afford to waste.

THE VILLAGES

Mountain villages are small enough that people often know one another's families.

Reputation matters.
So does usefulness.

Someone who repairs roofs, grows food, cares for animals, weaves cloth, works with timber, or helps during difficult weather becomes part of the community through repeated acts rather than formal titles.

People notice who contributes.
They also notice who does not.

THE HOMES

Mountain homes are built around practicality.

Timber and stone are common. Rooms are designed to retain heat. Kitchens are important because food preparation and preservation matter across the colder months.

Homes contain fewer things that exist only for display. The beauty of the region is often found in materials that have been used for a long time rather than objects that have been carefully arranged to impress.

THE WORK

Work is closely connected to survival.

Woodworking, farming, animal care, weaving, repair, trade, and seasonal labor all form part of mountain life.

Some work belongs to individuals.
Some work belongs to everyone.

A difficult task can become a community task simply because it is easier to complete together.

THE COMMUNITY

The mountains offer Elizabeth something she initially associates with freedom: fewer formal ceremonies, fewer servants, fewer people concerned with rank.

But community creates its own expectations.

People remember.
People notice.

People help each other and therefore acquire obligations to one another.

The absence of palace protocol does not mean the absence of social rules.

Elizabeth eventually begins to understand the difference.

THE GOVERNMENT

THE WORK BEHIND THE KINGDOM

Norwyn is not governed by the royal family alone. The Crown exists alongside ministers, civil servants, courts, financial institutions, administrative departments, and other state institutions responsible for running the kingdom.

Most government work is invisible to ordinary people. That is partly the point.

A citizen notices the road being repaired. They may not notice the meeting that approved the money.

A business notices a change in trade regulations. It may never see the document that produced it.

A treaty changes the conditions under which goods move between countries. Most people experience the result without ever meeting the people who negotiated it.

THE CROWN

The Crown represents continuity, national identity, and the monarchy's constitutional and ceremonial responsibilities.

The Queen receives foreign representatives, participates in state occasions, signs or approves documents where required, and represents Norwyn publicly.

But the Crown does not operate alone.

THE MINISTRIES

Ministries handle the practical responsibilities of the state.

They deal with finance, foreign affairs, trade, transportation, education, health, internal administration, and other areas of public life.

Ministers make decisions. Civil servants carry much of the work forward.

THE COURTS

The courts provide the formal institutions through which laws are interpreted and disputes are handled.

They belong to the kingdom's institutional structure rather than to the royal household.

THE TREASURY

The Treasury oversees the kingdom's finances and the management of public money.

Budgets, spending, taxation, debt, and major financial agreements eventually affect ordinary life.

FOREIGN AFFAIRS

Foreign affairs connects Norwyn to other countries, particularly Valenne.

Treaties, diplomatic meetings, trade negotiations, state visits, and international agreements are handled through this part of government.

Some of the decisions made here eventually reach Elizabeth's life without her knowing that they ever existed.

THE ROYAL FAMILY

A FAMILY THAT IS ALSO AN INSTITUTION

The royal family occupies an unusual position in Norwyn. They are a family. They are also an institution.

Their private decisions can carry public consequences.

A marriage can affect diplomacy. An inheritance can affect succession. A disagreement can become political if it concerns the future of the Crown.

Even an ordinary family decision can become complicated when the people involved are expected to represent an entire kingdom.

QUEEN ANNE

Queen Anne is Elizabeth's mother and the reigning Queen of Norwyn.

She has spent much of her life understanding responsibility as something that must be carried whether or not it is convenient.

She loves Elizabeth. She also has a clear idea of what Elizabeth needs to become.

For Anne, preparing Elizabeth for that life is an act of love. It is also an act of control. The two are not always easy to separate.

ELIZABETH

Elizabeth has been raised inside the royal household.

She understands etiquette, responsibility, ceremony, and the expectations attached to her position. She has also grown accustomed to a life in which many decisions have already been made around her.

Her question is not simply whether she wants to leave. It is whether she can recognize what she wants when nobody else is answering the question first.

EDWARD OF VALENNE

Edward is the man Elizabeth is expected to marry.

He understands the political circumstances surrounding their engagement, but he is not without personal feelings.

His relationship with Elizabeth becomes complicated precisely because the arrangement is not entirely false. There are genuine feelings inside a decision that was never entirely theirs to make.

THE ROYAL HOUSEHOLD

The household includes the people who work around the royal family every day.

Some have served the family for years. Some know Elizabeth better than the public ever could.

They see the difference between the woman Elizabeth is expected to be and the person she is when nobody is formally watching.

THE PUBLIC FAMILY

The royal family belongs partly to itself and partly to Norwyn.

Births are announced. Marriages are reported. Ceremonies are photographed. Clothing is discussed. Relationships become subjects of speculation.

The family learns to live with being observed. The people watching them learn to treat observation as ordinary.

NORWYN IN THE 1990s

A KINGDOM BETWEEN ERAS

Norwyn in the 1990s is modernizing, but it has not abandoned the systems that came before it. Television exists alongside newspapers. Landline telephones remain ordinary. Computers are becoming more common without yet being everywhere. Cars and buses fill streets designed decades earlier. Traditional businesses operate alongside newer industries.

Young people have access to music, films, books, and ideas that move across borders more quickly than they once did.

The monarchy carries its history into a decade in which many other parts of life are changing.

COMMUNICATION

Letters still matter. Telephone calls are common.

Newspapers remain influential. Television brings national events into people's homes.

Information moves faster than it once did, but it is still possible for a person to be unreachable.

TRANSPORT

Cars, buses, trains, river transport, and mountain roads connect the kingdom.

The capital is comparatively easy to navigate. The mountains are not.

Distance still determines who can visit whom and how quickly information can travel.

MEDIA

Newspapers report on politics, business, royal events, crime, culture, and society.

Television gives the monarchy another way to enter ordinary homes.

Royal life can therefore be both distant and strangely familiar. People may never meet Elizabeth and still feel that they know something about her.

FASHION

Clothing reflects class, occupation, age, location, and occasion.

Royal clothing is formal and carefully considered. Capital residents have greater access to changing fashion. Mountain communities favor clothing that survives work and weather.

A person's clothes can reveal where they belong before they say anything.

EVERYDAY LIFE

Schools open in the morning. Offices fill. Shops close at predictable hours.

Families eat together or separately depending on their circumstances. People read, watch television, listen to music, attend church or other religious services, meet friends, work, travel, shop, and argue.

Norwyn is not always dramatic. Most of the time, it is ordinary.

That is what makes the moments that change it matter.

THE NORWYN ARCHIVE

THE KINGDOM AS IT WAS RECORDED

Some parts of Norwyn are easier to understand through documents than descriptions. The archive collects pieces of the world that existed around the story.

MAPS

Maps of Norwyn, the capital, the mountain region, royal properties, major roads, and the border with Valenne.

The cartographic survey shows both sovereign roads and the unmapped passages of the northern range.

NEWSPAPERS

Royal announcements. Political developments. Business news. Society pages. Local events.

Advertisements.

The things Norwyn chose to print and the things it did not.

ROYAL DOCUMENTS

Invitations. Official announcements. Royal correspondence.

Household schedules. Ceremonial programs. Documents connected to the royal family.

GOVERNMENT RECORDS

Treaties. Trade agreements. Reports.

Official correspondence. Administrative documents. Public notices.

PERSONAL PAPERS

Letters. Notes. Photographs. Diaries.

Postcards.

Small records of people who were living through events rather than writing history.

EVERYDAY NORWYN

Menus. Train schedules. Shop advertisements. School notices.

Event programs. Newspaper advertisements. Packaging. Receipts.

Ordinary objects that reveal what life looked like when nobody was trying to preserve it.

PLACES IN THE NOVEL

WHERE THE STORY HAPPENS

The world of Or So She Thought is not contained inside one building. Each location places Elizabeth inside a different set of expectations.

THE ROYAL PALACE

Elizabeth's childhood home and the center of her family's public life.

The stone residence where protocol is quiet, pervasive, and learned before it is ever questioned.

THE ROYAL LIBRARY

A private space associated with reading, reflection, and some of Elizabeth's earliest questions.

Shelves that hold generations of recorded history alongside private, unread notebooks.

THE CAPITAL

The city surrounding the palace, where royal life meets ordinary life.

Stone quays, government avenues, crowded apartment blocks, and river trade.

THE RAG

An institution connected to Elizabeth's education and intellectual development.

The Royal Academy of Governance, where theories of state power are taught alongside quiet debate.

ELIAS'S WORKSHOP

A family workshop shaped by timber, iron, mechanical work, and independent labor.

A place where usefulness matters more than ceremony.

VALENNE

The neighboring kingdom Elizabeth is expected to enter through marriage.

A realm bound to Norwyn by trade, industrial wealth, and generational treaties.

THE MOUNTAINS

A region of Norwyn where Elizabeth encounters a different set of expectations and a different understanding of work, community, and responsibility.

Steep slopes, cold winters, communal ovens, and obligations shaped by survival.

MAMA ELENA'S HOME

A temporary home for Elizabeth outside the world she has always known.

It is not a place without rules. It is simply a place where she has to discover them for herself.
NORWYN IS MORE THAN A SETTING

Norwyn is the world that taught Elizabeth what was normal.

The palace taught her one version of responsibility.
The capital taught her what status looked like.
Her family taught her what a successful life was supposed to contain.
Valenne showed her how personal decisions could become political ones.
The mountains showed her that leaving one set of expectations does not mean entering a world without expectations.

And everywhere she goes, she carries something from the place she thought she had left behind.

Dramatis Personae

MEET THE PEOPLE OF NORWYN

Elizabeth's life is shaped by the people around her, but none of them can be reduced to what they want from her.

They love her.
They need things from her.
They protect her.
They misunderstand her.
Sometimes they do several of these things at once.

ELIZABETH

Elizabeth has spent most of her life knowing what is expected of her.

She is a daughter, a royal, a student, and a future bride. She has learned how to read a room, anticipate a response, and make herself appropriate to the circumstances around her.

What she has not yet learned is how to recognize what she wants before everyone else has had the opportunity to tell her.

ANNE

Queen Anne is Elizabeth's mother and one of the principal architects of the life prepared for her.

She does not believe she is taking Elizabeth's choices away.

She believes she is preparing her for a world that will not forgive her for being unprepared.

EDWARD OF VALENNE

Edward enters Elizabeth's life as the man she is expected to marry.

He is intelligent, observant, politically aware, and more difficult to understand than the arrangement between them initially suggests.

ELIAS

Elias is one of the first people to notice the difference between what Elizabeth says and what she has learned to say.

He is not her answer.

He has a life, loyalties, ambitions, and reasons of his own for being there.

ISABEL

Isabel has spent years serving the royal household and understands its rules from a position Elizabeth has rarely had reason to consider.

She knows what the palace asks of people.

She also knows that people sometimes choose to give it more than it asks.

MAMA ELENA

Far from the world Elizabeth knows, Mama Elena introduces her to another way of living—and another set of expectations.

The Mountain does not turn out to be a place without rules.

It simply has rules Elizabeth has never learned.

ROWAN

Rowan's influence reaches Elizabeth through ideas before it reaches her through memory.

His work raises a question that becomes increasingly difficult for her to ignore:

What does a person carry with them when they leave?

The Making of the Novel

WHERE IT BEGAN

Or So She Thought did not arrive fully formed. It changed as I wrote it.

Characters became more complicated. Questions became less convenient. Scenes survived that I expected to cut, while others disappeared after revealing that they belonged to an earlier version of the story.
This is a record of some of what changed along the way.

WHERE IT BEGAN

Every novel has a version of itself that existed before the version the reader eventually sees.

Or So She Thought began with a different story, and Elizabeth was not initially the person I thought she would become.

Somewhere along the way, her story became more interesting than the story I had originally intended to tell.

BUILDING NORWYN

Norwyn had to feel like a place that existed before Elizabeth began questioning it.

Its traditions could not exist simply to obstruct her. Its people had to have reasons for believing what they believed.

A world becomes more convincing when the people inside it do not think of themselves as characters in someone else's conflict.

BUILDING ELIZABETH

Elizabeth's development was never meant to be a simple movement from obedience to rebellion. The more I understood her, the less satisfying that story became.

She could love the life she had been given and still question it. She could leave and still carry it with her. She could return without becoming the same person who had left.

THE CHARACTERS WHO CHANGED

Characters rarely remain exactly where an author first puts them.

Some become more sympathetic. Some become less. Some become harder to judge at all.

THE SCENES THAT SURVIVED

Some scenes survive because they move the plot forward.

Others survive because removing them changes the meaning of everything around them. These are some of the scenes that refused to disappear.

THE SCENES THAT DIDN'T

Not every scene belongs in the finished novel.

Some exist only to teach the writer something the reader never needs to see.

THE TITLE

Or So She Thought became the title because certainty is part of Elizabeth's story.

The phrase belongs to the space between what a person believes and what they later discover was incomplete.

Questions & Archive

BEYOND THE NOVEL

The ideas, unresolved questions, and living archive from the world of Or So She Thought.

THE QUESTIONS BEHIND THE STORY

HOW DO YOU KNOW WHAT YOU WANT WHEN YOU WERE TAUGHT WHAT TO WANT?

A person can be influenced by family, culture, class, circumstance, and expectation without their desires becoming false.
So where does influence end and choice begin?

CAN A LIFE CHOSEN FOR YOU BECOME GENUINELY YOURS?

We inherit more than possessions.
We inherit assumptions about what a good life looks like, what is sensible, what is respectable, and what is possible.
Sometimes we eventually choose those things ourselves. Does their history make the choice less real?

CAN SOMEONE LOVE YOU AND STILL DECIDE TOO MUCH OF YOUR LIFE?

Love does not always arrive separately from power.
Sometimes the person protecting you is also the person deciding what you need protection from.

IS LEAVING ALWAYS FREEDOM?

Leaving changes what surrounds a person.
It does not necessarily change what that person has learned to expect, fear, value, or repeat.

WHAT DO WE CARRY WITH US WHEN WE LEAVE?

A person can cross a border, leave a house, change their clothes, abandon a role—and still bring much of the old world with them.

CAN RETURNING BE A CHOICE?

Sometimes leaving is necessary.
Sometimes staying is.
Sometimes the harder question is whether returning can ever mean going back freely.

WHAT HAPPENS WHEN NOBODY HAS TO TELL YOU NO?

The deepest rules are sometimes the ones we learn well enough to enforce ourselves.

WHAT DOES IT MEAN TO CHOOSE WHEN SO MUCH OF YOU WAS FORMED BEFORE YOU COULD CHOOSE?

Before a person can decide what they believe, they have already learned what to notice, what to expect, what to fear, and what to call normal.
Choice does not begin from nowhere.

The Author

ABOUT QUEEN USMAN

A novelist interested in how people become who they are.

Queen Usman writes fiction about the things that shape people before they fully understand they are being shaped—family, desire, expectation, relationships, power, circumstance, and repetition.

Her current novel, Or So She Thought, is a psychological novel set in 1990s Norwyn. It follows Elizabeth, a young woman whose future has been carefully prepared for her, and the increasingly difficult question of how much of that future she has actually chosen.

Her essays follow the questions her fiction leaves behind, looking at the ordinary ways people become who they are.
