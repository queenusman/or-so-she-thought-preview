The World

EXPLORE THE KINGDOM OF NORWYN

A kingdom shaped by inheritance, tradition, commerce, and the people who have learned how to live within them.

Norwyn is a kingdom of contrasts.

The capital is busy with commerce, government, diplomacy, and social life. The palace exists within the capital but operates according to rules of its own. Beyond the city, the mountains preserve ways of life shaped by colder weather, difficult geography, and generations of communal work.

Across the border lies Valenne, a neighboring kingdom whose relationship with Norwyn has been built through treaties, trade, diplomacy, and obligations older than Elizabeth herself.

Elizabeth has spent most of her life inside one version of Norwyn. The further she moves through it, the more she begins to notice how many versions there are.

01 — THE CAPITAL

WHERE THE KINGDOM MEETS ITSELF

Norwyn's capital was built along stone quays beside the river, where commerce, government, and public life have grown around one another for generations.

The waterfront begins early. Barges arrive carrying goods from elsewhere in the kingdom and beyond it. Workers unload cargo while merchants, clerks, and drivers begin the day's business. Further inland, government offices fill with civil servants. Shops open along the commercial streets. Newspapers appear at kiosks and cafés. Buses move through the older avenues, sharing the roads with cars that belong to a newer decade.

The city changes gradually as one district gives way to another. The buildings around the government quarter are formal and imposing. Commercial streets are louder, with shopfronts, restaurants, banks, hotels, and offices occupying buildings that have often served several purposes over their lifetimes. Residential neighborhoods range from large private houses and carefully maintained avenues to crowded districts where ordinary families live much closer together.

The palace sits within this city, but it does not belong entirely to it. The people of the capital know the royal family exists behind its walls. They see them at ceremonies, read about them in newspapers, hear about them through television broadcasts and conversations, and sometimes encounter the consequences of decisions made inside rooms they will never enter.

The capital has learned to live alongside the monarchy. For most people, it is not extraordinary. It is simply part of the city.

Goods arrive here before making their way into warehouses, shops, factories, restaurants, and private homes. The waterfront is practical rather than decorative: stone, iron, warehouses, loading areas, carts, vehicles, workers, river traffic, and businesses that depend on the movement of goods.

It is also one of the places where different parts of Norwyn meet. A shipment from the mountains can pass through the same streets as imported goods from Valenne. A merchant can conduct business within sight of a government building. A royal decision about trade can eventually become something as ordinary as the price of a product in a shop.

The river connects more of Norwyn than the people standing beside it necessarily realize.

Its buildings are formal because government is meant to appear permanent.

Inside them, however, much of the work is ordinary: clerks sort documents, officials attend meetings, ministers receive reports, budgets are discussed, letters are answered, and agreements are reviewed.

The kingdom is maintained through thousands of such decisions.

Banks stand beside shops. Hotels serve diplomats and visiting businessmen. Restaurants fill at lunchtime. Department stores display goods that would have been difficult to obtain in previous generations.

There are expensive establishments where wealth is expected and ordinary businesses where people come simply because they need something.

The capital does not divide neatly into rich and poor. Different kinds of wealth exist beside one another.

Some neighborhoods are quiet and affluent, with large houses, gardens, private schools, and established families. Others are densely populated, with apartments, small shops, schools, markets, and streets where people know one another because they have little choice but to share the same spaces.

The city changes depending on who is looking at it. A palace resident, a government clerk, a shopkeeper, a student, and a dock worker can all live in the same capital without experiencing quite the same city.

02 — THE PALACE

WHERE AUTHORITY BECOMES ORDINARY

The palace is the seat of the royal family and the center of ceremonial life in Norwyn. Its public rooms are designed for occasions that make authority visible: state dinners, diplomatic receptions, royal announcements, formal visits, ceremonies, and celebrations.

But the palace is not only the rooms shown to visitors. Behind the ceremonial spaces are private apartments, family rooms, offices, kitchens, staff corridors, storage rooms, gardens, archives, and the countless practical spaces required to keep a large household functioning.

There is a difference between the palace people visit and the palace people live in. Elizabeth knows the second one. She knows which doors are used by guests and which are used by staff. She knows which rooms require formality and which allow people to relax. She knows how quickly a conversation can change when someone important enters the room.

She has learned these things gradually. Nobody sat her down and explained all of them. She simply grew up knowing.

Everything in these rooms has a purpose. Where people sit matters. Who enters first matters. Who speaks first matters.

What is displayed on a table, wall, or mantel can carry meaning even when nobody mentions it aloud. The rooms are designed to make continuity visible.

Books accumulate across generations. Official documents sit alongside private reading. Correspondence may be kept nearby. Family records and political material can exist within the same walls.

For Elizabeth, the library is one of the places where the palace feels less like an institution and more like a home. That distinction is not always easy to maintain.

The private wing contains the rooms in which the royal family can temporarily stop performing the public version of themselves.

It is where conversations happen without an audience. It is also where some of the most important decisions are made.

Household staff manage meals, clothing, schedules, rooms, correspondence, gardens, transport, security, and countless details that most visitors never notice.

Some staff have worked for the family for years. They know routines. They recognize moods. They know which family members prefer silence in the morning and which expect conversation. They know when an event has gone badly before anyone says so.

Their knowledge is part of the palace's invisible structure.

The royal archives preserve what the institution chooses to remember.

It can also reveal what earlier generations were trying to manage, protect, or conceal.

03 — VALENNE

THE KINGDOM BEYOND THE BORDER

Valenne is Norwyn's neighboring kingdom and one of its most important political and economic partners. Its relationship with Norwyn began long before Elizabeth was born. Treaties connect the two kingdoms. Trade connects their businesses. Diplomacy connects their governments. Previous agreements continue to shape decisions being made in the present.

Elizabeth's proposed marriage therefore does not exist in isolation. It is personal. It is also part of a relationship between two countries that has been developing for generations.

Valenne has its own history, institutions, customs, social hierarchy, and expectations. It is not simply another version of Norwyn, and Edward is not simply a man who happens to come from across the border. He belongs to a different political and social world.

Its political priorities do not always correspond perfectly with Norwyn's. That difference matters when agreements are negotiated.

A treaty can benefit both countries while benefiting them in different ways.

Businesses depend on agreements between the kingdoms. Financial institutions follow political developments. Industries benefit from stable relations.

A royal marriage can therefore attract attention for reasons that have little to do with romance.

The relationship between the kingdoms is built on more than friendship. There are agreements, obligations, economic interests, and history.

And there are things each kingdom would prefer the other not to misunderstand.

Edward has grown up understanding his position within this bilateral accord. He knows that marriage can be personal and political at the same time.

He also knows that Elizabeth is being asked to enter a world that existed before she was asked to enter it. For Elizabeth, understanding Edward eventually means understanding what comes with him.

04 — THE MOUNTAINS

A DIFFERENT NORWYN

The mountains are part of Norwyn, but they can feel like another country. The weather is colder. Roads are more difficult. Villages are smaller. Work is more visible because survival in a difficult landscape leaves little room for pretending that things maintain themselves.

Wood has to be gathered. Buildings have to be repaired. Food has to be stored. Animals have to be cared for. People depend on one another.

The mountains have preserved ways of life that have changed more slowly than those of the capital.

But they have not preserved a world without rules. They simply have different ones.

Distance matters here. A place that looks close on a map may take hours to reach.

Weather can change plans. Geography shapes what people can build, grow, transport, and afford to waste.

Mountain villages are small enough that people often know one another's families.

Someone who repairs roofs, grows food, cares for animals, weaves cloth, works with timber, or helps during difficult weather becomes part of the community through repeated acts rather than formal titles.

People notice who contributes. They also notice who does not.

Mountain homes are built around practicality. Timber and stone are common. Rooms are designed to retain heat. Kitchens are important because food preparation and preservation matter across the colder months.

Homes contain fewer things that exist only for display. The beauty of the region is often found in materials that have been used for a long time rather than objects that have been carefully arranged to impress.

Work is closely connected to survival. Some work belongs to individuals; some work belongs to everyone.

A difficult task can become a community task simply because it is easier to complete together.

The mountains offer Elizabeth something she initially associates with freedom: fewer formal ceremonies, fewer servants, fewer people concerned with rank.

But community creates its own expectations. People remember. People notice. People help each other and therefore acquire obligations to one another.

The absence of palace protocol does not mean the absence of social rules. Elizabeth eventually begins to understand the difference.

05 — THE GOVERNMENT

THE WORK BEHIND THE KINGDOM

Norwyn is not governed by the royal family alone. The Crown exists alongside ministers, civil servants, courts, financial institutions, administrative departments, and other state institutions responsible for running the kingdom.

Most government work is invisible to ordinary people. That is partly the point.

A citizen notices the road being repaired. They may not notice the meeting that approved the money. A business notices a change in trade regulations. It may never see the document that produced it. A treaty changes the conditions under which goods move between countries. Most people experience the result without ever meeting the people who negotiated it.

The Crown represents continuity, national identity, and the monarchy's constitutional responsibilities. The Queen receives foreign representatives, participates in state occasions, and signs documents where required.

The monarchy is not the entire state; it operates within established covenants.

Ministries handle the practical responsibilities of the state. Ministers make decisions, while civil servants carry much of the actual administrative work forward.

The courts belong to the kingdom's institutional structure rather than to the royal household, maintaining the rule of law across all social tiers.

The Treasury oversees public finance. Budgets, taxation, spending allocations, and trade subsidies determined here eventually dictate the price of daily goods in market stalls.

Treaties, diplomatic summits, trade concessions, and state visits are formulated here. Some of the international agreements made inside these rooms directly shape Elizabeth's life before she is ever informed of their existence.

06 — THE ROYAL FAMILY

A FAMILY THAT IS ALSO AN INSTITUTION

The royal family occupies an unusual position in Norwyn. They are a family. They are also an institution.

Their private decisions can carry public consequences.

A marriage can affect diplomacy. An inheritance can affect succession. A disagreement can become political if it concerns the future of the Crown.

Even an ordinary family decision can become complicated when the people involved are expected to represent an entire kingdom.

Authority is experienced as an inherited burden. To those inside it, maintaining the stability of the realm is an obligation that overrides private preferences.

Succession determines how public continuity is maintained across generations. From childhood, potential heirs learn that their futures belong to the kingdom before they belong to themselves.

A royal union is scrutinized by cabinets, foreign ministries, and the press. It is negotiated to protect borders, reassure allies, and ensure dynastic survival.

Births are announced. Marriages are reported. Ceremonies are photographed. Clothing is analyzed. The family learns to live with being observed, while the public learns to treat observation as ordinary.

07 — NORWYN IN THE 1990s

A KINGDOM BETWEEN ERAS

Norwyn in the 1990s is modernizing, but it has not abandoned the systems that came before it.

Television exists alongside newspapers. Landline telephones remain ordinary. Computers are becoming more common without yet being everywhere. Cars and buses fill streets designed decades earlier. Traditional businesses operate alongside newer industries.

Young people have access to music, films, books, and ideas that move across borders more quickly than they once did.

The monarchy carries its history into a decade in which many other parts of life are changing.

Newspapers remain influential. Television brings national events directly into people's living rooms. Information moves faster than it once did, but it is still entirely possible for a person to be unreachable.

The capital is comparatively easy to navigate; the mountains are not. Distance still determines who can visit whom and how quickly news can travel across county lines.

Newspapers report on politics, business, royal events, culture, and society. Television gives the Crown another way to enter ordinary homes, making royal figures feel both distant and strangely familiar.

Royal clothing is formal and carefully considered. Capital residents have access to modern fashion trends, while mountain communities favor wool and canvas that survive harsh weather. A person's clothes reveal their world before they speak.

Families eat together or separately depending on their circumstances. People work, read, listen to radio, attend religious services, meet friends, shop, and argue. Norwyn is not always dramatic; most of the time, it is ordinary. That is what makes the moments that disrupt it matter.

09 — PLACES IN THE NOVEL

WHERE THE STORY HAPPENS

The world of Or So She Thought is not contained inside one building. Each location places Elizabeth inside a different set of expectations.

The seat of the royal family where authority is quiet, routine, and structured by etiquette learned before anyone thought to explain why.

Shelves that hold generations of recorded state history alongside private, unread notebooks and forbidden conversations.

Stone quays, government avenues, crowded apartment blocks, river trade, and the bustling rhythm of commerce.

The Royal Academy of Governance, where theories of constitutional state power are taught alongside quiet, dangerous debates.

A place where usefulness matters more than ceremony, and where broken things can be honestly repaired.

An industrial realm bound to Norwyn by trade, mineral wealth, and generational political covenants.

Steep slopes, cold winters, communal ovens, and obligations shaped by shared survival rather than royal birthright.

It is not a place without rules. It is simply a place where she has to discover them for herself.

Dramatis Personae

MEET THE PEOPLE OF NORWYN

Elizabeth's life is shaped by the people around her, but none of them can be reduced to what they want from her.

They love her.
They need things from her.
They protect her.
They misunderstand her.
Sometimes they do several of these things at once.

ELIZABETH

Elizabeth has spent most of her life knowing what is expected of her.

She is a daughter, a royal, a student, and a future bride. She has learned how to read a room, anticipate a response, and understand what a situation requires of her.

For most of her life, this has been useful. It has also made certain questions harder to answer.

ANNE

Queen Anne is Elizabeth's mother.

She has spent years preparing her daughter for a life in which very little can be treated as purely personal. She believes preparation is a form of protection. She does not always recognize when the two begin to become difficult to separate.

EDWARD OF VALENNE

Edward enters Elizabeth's life as the man she is expected to marry.

He is intelligent, observant, politically aware, and more complicated than the arrangement between them initially suggests. The marriage has consequences beyond the two people expected to enter it.

ELIAS

Elias comes from a world outside the palace, but his work brings him into places Elizabeth has spent most of her life taking for granted.

He is observant, independent, and not easily impressed by the distinctions that matter inside the royal household.

ISABEL

Isabel is Elizabeth's younger sister, and although they were raised within the same royal household, they have not experienced it in quite the same way.

She has her own understanding of what their family owes to the kingdom, and her own reasons for accepting some things Elizabeth has begun to question.

MAMA ELENA

Mama Elena lives far from the world Elizabeth has known.

Her life is shaped by work, community, necessity, and expectations of a different kind. Elizabeth does not find a place without rules; she finds people whose rules she has never learned.

ROWAN

Rowan's influence reaches Elizabeth through a book before it reaches her through memory.

His ideas give language to questions Elizabeth has not yet learned how to ask.

THE MAKING OF THE NOVEL

WHERE IT BEGAN

The Making of Or So She Thought

Or So She Thought did not arrive fully formed.

There was a version of the story before there was this one. There were characters who belonged to earlier drafts, places that existed before I understood what they were for, scenes that seemed essential until they weren't, and questions I thought I was writing about before realizing I had been asking a different question all along.

Novels are built through accumulation, revision, disagreement, accident, and the slow realization that the story you thought you were writing is no longer the most interesting one available. Or So She Thought changed because I kept following those changes.

THE STORY BEFORE ELIZABETH

On the story that existed before Or So She Thought, and the moment Elizabeth became more interesting than the novel I had originally planned.

Every novel begins somewhere else. At first, I was more interested in external events and political maneuvering across borders. Then Elizabeth began to emerge—not as an instrument of the plot, but as a person whose quiet compliance was itself an exhausting form of labor. Once her inner life became the primary question, the entire architecture of the book had to shift to meet her.

THE MENDED BLOUSE

On a small object that unexpectedly became part of Elizabeth's story.

Some objects in fiction are chosen deliberately for their symbolism; others arrive by accident and refuse to leave. In early drafts, the blouse was merely a detail in a domestic scene. But watching someone whose clothes have always been selected, tailored, and maintained by others attempt to fix an imperfect hem became a quiet study in what happens when self-reliance begins with clumsy fingers.

THE TITLE

On how Or So She Thought became the title, and why uncertainty mattered more than certainty.

Titles often attempt to declare what a book is about. Or So She Thought does the opposite—it creates hesitation. It belongs to the narrow space between what a person assumes to be true and what they slowly realize was incomplete. It is not about discovering that everything was a lie; it is about discovering how much more complicated the truth actually is.

BUILDING NORWYN

On creating a kingdom that had to feel lived in before Elizabeth ever began questioning it.

A world cannot feel like a stage set constructed merely for the protagonist's dilemma. Norwyn’s ministries, stone quays, river barges, and mountain passes had to exist as places where ordinary people live their ordinary days. If the rules of the kingdom did not make coherent sense to the people upholding them, Elizabeth's quiet resistance would have cost her nothing.

WRITING ANNE

On writing a mother whose love, certainty, responsibility, and mistakes could not be separated neatly from one another.

The easiest version of Anne would have been an imposing tyrant. But power inside families rarely operates through overt hostility. Anne is driven by duty and a fierce belief that discipline is the highest form of protection. When control looks and feels like genuine care, the boundaries between devotion and constraint become almost impossible to disentangle.

THE SCENES THAT SURVIVED

On the scenes that remained after earlier versions of the novel disappeared.

As a manuscript is rewritten, whole chapters, characters, and subplots fall away. The scenes that endure are rarely the loudest ones. They are the moments of silence: a conversation in a carriage where nothing of consequence is said, an unread letter left on a cedar desk, or a shared glance across a banquet hall that registers an unspoken change.
QUESTIONS THE NOVEL LEAVES BEHIND

BEYOND THE NOVEL

Or So She Thought is a story, but some of the questions it raises do not belong entirely to Elizabeth, Norwyn, or the world of the novel.

They appear in ordinary lives too.

How much of what we call preference was learned?
How much of what feels inevitable was simply repeated?
When does something we were taught become something we genuinely believe?
And what happens when we begin to notice?
THE QUESTIONS

HOW DO YOU KNOW WHAT YOU WANT?

Family, culture, circumstance, expectation, and experience all participate in forming a person.
But influence does not make a desire false.
So where does influence end and choice begin?

CAN A LIFE CHOSEN FOR YOU BECOME YOUR OWN?

We inherit ideas about what a good life should look like.
Sometimes we reject them.
Sometimes we keep them.
Sometimes we cannot tell which happened.

CAN LOVE AND CONTROL EXIST TOGETHER?

The person who knows you best may also be the person who feels most entitled to decide what is best for you.
Where does care become something else?

WHAT HAPPENS WHEN A RULE BECOMES ORDINARY?

Some rules remain visible.
Others become so familiar that nobody thinks to call them rules anymore.

WHEN DOES A RESPONSE BECOME A TRAIT?

A person does something often enough.
Other people begin to expect it.
Eventually, the person may begin to expect it too.

WHAT DO WE CALL CHOICE WHEN THE OPTIONS ALREADY FEEL OBVIOUS?

A decision can feel completely voluntary and still have a long history behind it.
That does not make it meaningless.
It makes its history worth examining.

The Author

ABOUT QUEEN USMAN

A clinical medical student and novelist interested in how people become who they are.

Queen Usman is a Nigerian clinical medical student and novelist. She writes fiction about the things that shape people before they fully understand they are being shaped: family, desire, expectation, relationships, power, circumstance, and repetition[cite: 1].

Her current novel, Or So She Thought, is a psychological novel set in 1990s Norwyn. It follows Elizabeth, a young woman whose future has been carefully prepared for her, as the life she has always understood begins to become less certain.

Her medical training has sharpened her interest in observation, human behavior, and the ways people are shaped by the worlds they inhabit. She is particularly interested in the space between what happens to a person and what that person eventually becomes.

Her essays follow the questions her fiction leaves behind, looking at the ordinary ways people acquire expectations, habits, assumptions, preferences, and ways of seeing.
